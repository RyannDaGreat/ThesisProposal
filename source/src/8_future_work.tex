Peekaboo's impact came from demonstrating how image diffusion foundation models can do more than generate images, instead using them for zero shot referring segmentation. And it does this by treating it as a black box that just predicts noise, never pulling apart its attention layers or retraining any weights. The same optimization that powers Peekaboo's alpha-mask search became the engine of Diffusion Illusions (\cref{chapter:diffillusions}), and the broader observation has since been picked up across zero-shot segmentation, including in work like DiffCut~\cite{couairon2024diffcut}. Peekaboo was also, to my knowledge, the first attempt at transparent (RGBA) generation from an RGB-only diffusion model, albeit at low quality, more than a year before the first dedicated RGBA generator, LayerDiffuse~\cite{zhang2024layerdiffuse}, was trained.
% It is cited by both of the diffusion-as-classifier papers that made the same point from the discriminative side: \citet{li2023diffusionclassifier}\AI{, which turns a frozen text-to-image diffusion model into a zero-shot image classifier by scoring how well each candidate label denoises the image,} and \citet{clark2023texttoimage}\AI{, which independently shows the same models act as strong zero-shot classifiers across standard benchmarks}, with 355 and 171 citations respectively as of June 2026.

Diffusion Illusions was one of the most well known works in my thesis. It was built on technological foundations of Peekaboo: it parametrized images with fourier feature networks, and used score distillation loss to optimize them. But it had a different goal, which was to create optical illusions, some of which humans would have little hope of designing by hand. In particular, the overlay illusions which reveal hidden images when superimposed on each other and rotated would be monstrously difficult for a human artist to do by hand.

Diffusion Illusions ended up triggering it's own small subfield of research: generating illusions via diffusion foundation models: every one of the papers in this paragraph was directly inspired by and builds on our work.
Visual Anagrams~\cite{geng2023visualanagrams}, a CVPR~2024 Oral, provided a way to generate a subset of illusions that can be represented by invertible, orthogonal transforms (i.e. rotations, puzzles with multiple solutions, or color inversions that turn one image into another). Factorized Diffusion~\cite{geng2024factorized} pushed the idea into frequency-decomposed hybrid images that show different subjects when viewing the image, and was used on the back of the official shirts for CVPR 2024.
  PTDiffusion~\cite{gao2025ptdiffusion} hid images through training-free phase transfer, such as a cliff face that simultaneously looks like a face.
  Illusion3D~\cite{feng2024illusion3d} and the CVPR~2025 Oral LookingGlass~\cite{chang2025lookingglass} created 3D diffusion illusions - using differentiable rendering, so that different lenses and mirrors placed on one subject (i.e. a castle) reveal another hidden subject (i.e. a lion) when viewed from a certain angle.
It's also been used as a benchmark for SDS-alternative algorithms~\cite{mcallister2024sdsbridge}, instead of just benchmarking for 3D reconstruction.


Diffusion Illusions itself was published, presented at, and also had a demo session at SIGGRAPH~2024. It also won the Best Demo Award at CVPR~2023, and became the subject of two videos by the science-communication YouTubers Steve Mould and Matt Parker - amassing over a million views.

Papers like Trans-Adapter~\cite{dai2025transadapter}, TKG-DM~\cite{tkgdm2025}, and PrismLayers~\cite{prismlayers2025} train or evaluate on it directly, and its images make up a good chunk of the LayerBench benchmark. Go-with-the-Flow, a CVPR~2025 Oral, is my other most impactful work and the most widely adopted method in this thesis. It was the best motion-control model at the time of release by a clear margin, and it held that lead for several months, largely because it was so simple to set up: once the noise warping code was complete, training was as simple as swapping out one noise for another and fine-tuning. As a result, I was able to train GWTF on CogVideoX-5B the week it dropped, when every other paper released for months afterwards was still using AnimateDiff. This simplicity is why the open-source community picked it up so fast: within weeks, the release passed a thousand GitHub stars, people had ported it to other backbones like AnimateDiff and HunyuanVideo, and others wrapped it in interactive tools and ComfyUI nodes for noise warping. 

It has since become the standard rendering bridge for simulation-to-video work: papers that compute motion some other way and then hand it to GWTF to render as photoreal video. WonderPlay~\cite{wonderplay2025} drives it with an interactive physics simulator, and PSIVG~\cite{psivg2026} puts a physical simulator in the generation loop; NewtonGen~\cite{newtongen2026} feeds it neural Newtonian dynamics; VLIPP~\cite{vlipp2025} plans physically plausible motion with a vision-language model; VideoFrom3D~\cite{videofrom3d2025} renders camera trajectories through 3D scene geometry; and FOFPred~\cite{fofpred2026} drives it with predicted future optical flow. For each of these papers GWTF is the backend renderer, and RealWonder~\cite{realwonder2026} goes a step further, re-implementing the warping inside its own real-time model.

MotionV2V is my most recent paper, which was accepted to CVPR~2026. It's main impact came from defining a new task. It was the first paper to do true motion video editing - where the entire video context, without discarding human-chosen chunks of information (i.e. inpainting or first-frame-to-video). I think motion edits are a class of edit that will eventually have to become mainstream: if we want to be able to manipulate any video without laborious inpainting, and be able to preserve arbitrary appearance content (such as an actor's facial expression), I would argue this is the most pure problem formulation for that.

It has around 11 citations right now. There are other papers currently being worked on that have the same goal of motion editing, which will come out later this year.

\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{chapter:conclusion}

% --- RYAN'S ORIGINAL NOTE ---
% [restate intro here basically]
% Foundation models are very powerful for many tasks. But they aren't useful if
% we stick to just text to image or text to video. Our ability to harness and
% control these models is what makes them useful, and is the focus of my work.
\AI{Foundation models are extraordinarily powerful, but they are not actually useful if all we do with them is text-to-image or text-to-video. The user types a short description and the model invents everything else, the composition, transparency, motion, semantics, out of its own prior, and that leaves a gap between what the user wanted and what they get. My argument throughout this thesis is that closing that gap is what makes these models useful, and that the structure you need to close it is already sitting inside the frozen model, waiting to be controlled. That is the focus of my work. The five methods here, Peekaboo, Diffusion Illusions, MAGICK, Go-with-the-Flow, and MotionV2V, each take control of a different axis, spatial semantics, multi-view geometry, transparency, temporal motion, and motion editing, and together they show that a frozen or lightly fine-tuned diffusion model can be steered toward things its training objective never had in mind.}

\AI{Going forward, I want to push this line of work from controlling video \emph{generation} to controlling video \emph{editing} in general. On the image side this already exists: a model like Nano Banana takes an image plus a natural-language instruction and makes exactly the edit you ask for, swap an object, change the background, fix the lighting, while leaving the rest of the image alone. Nothing like that really exists for video yet; editing a clip still mostly means regenerating it, which throws away the temporal coherence and detail of the original. MotionV2V (\cref{chapter:motionv2v}) is an early step toward this for the specific case of motion, and what I want to prove is that you can turn a general image-to-image editor into a general video-to-video editor, basically Nano Banana but for video, that handles any edit, appearance, motion, or both, without disturbing what you did not ask it to change. If that works, it would make video as directly editable as text instructions have made images, and it is the direction I plan to take this research.}
