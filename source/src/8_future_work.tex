\section{Peekaboo}

% --- RYAN'S ORIGINAL NOTE ---
% Peekaboo:
% this work was impactful because it showed people that diffusion foundation
% models could be used, even as a black box that predicts noise and without
% pulling apart the attention layers etc, to do more than just image generation.
% It inspired well cited papers such as the "your diffusion model is secretly a
% classifier" paper which was extremely well cited [ but I am unfamiliar so
% please take a look ]. It was also the first work to demonstrate RGBA
% text-to-image generation, albeit at low quality. The mechanism behind this
% paper's optimization was also directly used for diffusion illusions
% imeediateyly after the paper was written.
% [FACT-CHECK: "inspired" -> "cited by" (bib-only); "first RGBA" -> "first to
%  attempt"; "immediately after" -> ~13mo later. See impact_verified.md sec 1.]
\AI{Peekaboo's impact came from demonstrating how image diffusion foundation models can do more than generate images, instead using them for zero shot referring segmentation. And it does this by treating it as a black box that just predicts noise, never pulling apart its attention layers or retraining a single weight. The same optimization that powers Peekaboo's alpha-mask search became the engine of Diffusion Illusions (\cref{chapter:diffillusions}), and the broader observation has since been picked up across zero-shot segmentation, including in work like DiffCut~\cite{couairon2024diffcut}. Peekaboo was also, to my knowledge, the first attempt at transparent (RGBA) generation from an RGB-only diffusion model, albeit at low quality, more than a year before anyone trained a dedicated RGBA generator. It is cited by both of the diffusion-as-classifier papers that made the same point from the discriminative side,} \citet{li2023diffusionclassifier} \AI{and} \citet{clark2023texttoimage}\AI{.}

\AI{Diffusion Illusions is the most visible work in this thesis, and it essentially kicked off its own little subfield: using foundation models to generate optical illusions. It was published at SIGGRAPH~2024, won the Demo Award at CVPR~2023, and the illusions ended up as the subject of videos by the science-communication YouTubers Steve Mould and Matt Parker, both of whom credit the work directly. Before it, making this kind of illusion meant hand-crafted tricks or fiddly GAN pipelines; we showed that you could just optimize a shared image through a frozen diffusion model and get physically printable illusions across a whole range of classical families, automatically.}

\AI{It triggered a steady run of follow-ups. Visual Anagrams~\cite{geng2023visualanagrams}, a CVPR~2024 Oral, gave a simpler zero-shot take on view-conditioned illusions; Factorized Diffusion~\cite{geng2024factorized} pushed the idea into frequency-decomposed hybrid images; PTDiffusion~\cite{gao2025ptdiffusion} hid images through training-free phase transfer; and the whole thing jumped from 2D into 3D with Illusion3D~\cite{feng2024illusion3d} and the CVPR~2025 Oral LookingGlass~\cite{chang2025lookingglass}, which does anamorphic 3D illusions. Together they make up a small but clearly recognizable cluster of diffusion-based illusion work that I think Diffusion Illusions helped seed.}

\AI{When we released it, MAGICK was the largest general-domain RGBA image dataset available to the public, by a wide margin: the previous largest released general-domain matting set had only a few hundred objects, and in fact MAGICK shows more RGBA examples in its own figures than that dataset had in total. No bigger one has come out since, so it has quietly become the go-to dataset for transparent and layered image generation. Papers like Trans-Adapter~\cite{dai2025transadapter}, TKG-DM~\cite{tkgdm2025}, and PrismLayers~\cite{prismlayers2025} train or evaluate on it directly, and its images make up a good chunk of the LayerBench benchmark. MAGICK was a standard conference poster, so its impact does not really show up in citation counts or press; it shows up in people actually using the data, which they do thousands of times a month.}

\AI{Go-with-the-Flow, a CVPR~2025 Oral, is my other most impactful work and the most widely adopted method in this thesis. On our benchmarks it was the best motion-control model at the time by a clear margin, and it held that lead for months, largely because it was so simple to set up: all I really had to do was swap out one noise for another and fine-tune. That meant I could jump on a new base model like CogVideoX-5B the week it dropped, with no architectural surgery. The same simplicity is why the open-source community picked it up so fast. The release passed a thousand GitHub stars, and within weeks people had ported it to other backbones like AnimateDiff and HunyuanVideo and wrapped it in interactive tools and ComfyUI nodes for noise warping. It has since become a building block for things well outside motion transfer, like WonderPlay~\cite{wonderplay2025}, which uses it to render physically simulated scenes.}

\AI{Beyond the motion-control application, people have kept building on, and improving, the warped noise itself. EquiVDM~\cite{liu2025equivdm} works out the theory of why warped noise behaves the way it does, casting it in terms of equivariance, and others have carried it into new settings, including warping the noise around 3D camera geometry for camera-controllable generation~\cite{cameranoise2026}. Separately, Yosun Chang and I used the warped-noise sampler to build an interactive demo at CVPR~2025 that won a Best Demo Honorable Mention.}

\AI{MotionV2V is my most recent work, accepted to CVPR~2026, so it is still too new to say where its impact lands. What it does is demonstrate a new class of edit: motion editing. Earlier methods could change appearance or follow a trajectory you hand them~\cite{motioneditor,motionfollower,revideo2024}, but MotionV2V is, as far as I know, the first to do true video-to-video motion editing on arbitrary objects, reaching into the motion already present in a real video and changing it while keeping the content, instead of regenerating the scene or only handling human skeletons. I think motion edits are a class of edit that will eventually have to become mainstream: if we want to be able to manipulate any video, we will need them. Even this early, it has already started picking up citations from follow-up work on trajectory-based video editing.}


\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{chapter:conclusion}

% --- RYAN'S ORIGINAL NOTE ---
% [restate intro here basically]
% Foundation models are very powerful for many tasks. But they aren't useful if
% we stick to just text to image or text to video. Our ability to harness and
% control these models is what makes them useful, and is the focus of my work.
\AI{Foundation models are extraordinarily powerful, but they are not actually useful if all we do with them is text-to-image or text-to-video. The user types a short description and the model invents everything else, the composition, transparency, motion, semantics, out of its own prior, and that leaves a gap between what the user wanted and what they get. My argument throughout this thesis is that closing that gap is what makes these models useful, and that the structure you need to close it is already sitting inside the frozen model, waiting to be controlled. That is the focus of my work. The five methods here, Peekaboo, Diffusion Illusions, MAGICK, Go-with-the-Flow, and MotionV2V, each take control of a different axis, spatial semantics, multi-view geometry, transparency, temporal motion, and motion editing, and together they show that a frozen or lightly fine-tuned diffusion model can be steered toward things its training objective never had in mind.}

\section{Future Work}

% --- RYAN'S ORIGINAL NOTE ---
% Future work:
% In the future, I'll be working on video editing tasks - such as proof that an
% image-to-image general editor can be turned into a video-to-video general
% editor (like nanobanana but for video, look it up so you understand what that
% means claude).
% [Nano Banana = Gemini 2.5 Flash Image: a single general-purpose model that
%  takes an image + a text instruction and makes a targeted edit while preserving
%  the rest. The future-work goal: the same idea, but for video.]
\AI{Going forward, I want to push this line of work from controlling video \emph{generation} to controlling video \emph{editing} in general. On the image side this already exists: a model like Nano Banana takes an image plus a natural-language instruction and makes exactly the edit you ask for, swap an object, change the background, fix the lighting, while leaving the rest of the image alone. Nothing like that really exists for video yet; editing a clip still mostly means regenerating it, which throws away the temporal coherence and detail of the original. MotionV2V (\cref{chapter:motionv2v}) is an early step toward this for the specific case of motion, and what I want to prove is that you can turn a general image-to-image editor into a general video-to-video editor, basically Nano Banana but for video, that handles any edit, appearance, motion, or both, without disturbing what you did not ask it to change. If that works, it would make video as directly editable as text instructions have made images, and it is the direction I plan to take this research.}
